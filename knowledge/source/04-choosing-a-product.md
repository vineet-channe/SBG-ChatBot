# Choosing the right financing

## Quick guide by need
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/

| What you need | Usually the best fit | Also consider |
| A recurring cash gap (payroll, slow weeks, restocking) | Business line of credit | Working capital, revenue-based financing |
| One defined operating gap with a fixed payoff | Working capital | Term loan |
| A specific investment (renovation, expansion, second location) | Small business term loan | SBA 7(a) |
| Machinery, vehicles or technology | Equipment financing | Term loan |
| Cash tied up in unpaid B2B invoices | Invoice financing | Line of credit |
| A gap until a closing or known payout | Bridge capital | Line of credit |
| Buying a business or real estate | SBA 7(a) | Bridge capital while SBA closes |
| Money today for an emergency | Revenue-based financing | Working capital, line of credit |
| Lowest cost with home equity available | Business HELOC | SBA 7(a) |

If you know exactly what you need and how much it costs, a term loan is typically the right fit. If the need is ongoing or unpredictable, a line of credit is usually cheaper and more flexible.

## Term loan or line of credit?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/line-of-credit-calculator/

A term loan provides a lump sum upfront repaid on a fixed schedule; it suits one known amount with a defined purpose and a fixed payoff. A line of credit gives revolving access you draw from, repay and draw again, paying only on what you have drawn; it suits recurring or unpredictable costs such as payroll between invoices or seasonal inventory. If you can name the amount and the date you need it by, a term loan is usually the cheaper structure.

## Working capital or line of credit?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/working-capital-loans/

Working capital delivers a lump sum with a fixed repayment schedule, best when you know how much you need and want a defined payoff timeline. A line of credit is best for ongoing or variable needs. If the working capital need is a one-time, defined amount, working capital is simpler; if it recurs and varies, a line of credit is more efficient.

## Revenue-based financing or a term loan?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/resources/factor-rate-vs-interest-rate/

Revenue-based financing is priced with a factor rate and repaid from sales through small daily or weekly payments; approval rests mostly on revenue, and it funds fast, but it usually costs more and early repayment does not reduce the fixed total. Term loans and lines of credit use interest rates that accrue on the outstanding balance, so paying early can save money. RBF fits short-term, urgent or uneven-revenue needs; interest-rate products fit longer-term, planned investments.

## SBA 7(a) or conventional financing?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/sba-loans/

SBA financing offers larger amounts, longer terms and lower rates, but it needs more documentation and typically takes 30 to 90 days. SBG's conventional products fund in as little as 24 hours with minimal documentation. SBA is usually better for acquisitions, large capital needs and situations where long-term cost matters more than speed. Conventional financing is better for urgent needs, smaller amounts, or businesses that do not meet SBA criteria. Bridge capital can cover an acquisition deposit while SBA financing closes.

## Equipment financing or a term loan?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/equipment-financing/

Both provide a lump sum with fixed repayment. Equipment financing is secured by the asset, needs no other collateral, often has more flexible approval and runs 2 to 5 years matched to the equipment's useful life. A term loan runs 6 to 48 months and can be used for any purpose.

## Invoice financing or a line of credit?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/invoice-financing/

Invoice financing advances cash against specific unpaid commercial invoices; a line of credit gives general revolving working capital. They can be used together: many businesses use invoice financing for their largest invoice gaps and a line of credit for day-to-day cash flow.

## Bridge capital or a line of credit?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/bridge-capital/

A line of credit covers ongoing or recurring needs. Bridge capital is a single, defined advance for a specific gap with a known repayment event. If the gap is one-time and time-bounded with a clear exit, bridge capital is the right tool.

## HELOC or a home equity loan?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/home-equity-line-of-credit-heloc/

A HELOC is revolving: draw, repay and draw again, paying interest only on the outstanding balance. A home equity loan is a lump sum with fixed repayment, like a term loan. For ongoing or variable needs a HELOC is more flexible; for one specific purchase a home equity loan may fit better.

## Financing with bad credit
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/bad-credit-business-loans/

SBG accepts FICO scores from 500 across its products (600 for a HELOC). Approvals in the 500 to 600 range rest on cash flow and deposit history. Invoice financing and equipment financing are often the most accessible with imperfect credit, because approval leans on customers' credit or on the asset. Rates vary with the overall profile; the way to see a specific rate is to apply, which takes 5 minutes and does not affect credit.

## Financing without collateral
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/no-collateral-business-loans/

Most SBG products are fully unsecured: term loans, lines of credit, bridge capital, working capital and revenue-based financing require no collateral. Three products can involve collateral: equipment financing (the equipment), a HELOC (your home) and SBA 7(a) (may be required by the lender). Unsecured financing may still involve a UCC filing, a public notice of a security interest in business assets; it does not attach to your home, car or personal property. A personal guarantee may apply depending on the product and amount; your advisor explains the terms before you sign.

## Emergency and fast funding
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/emergency-business-loans/

For equipment failures, payroll shortfalls, supply disruptions, unexpected taxes or delayed receivables, SBG makes same-day decisions on most applications. If you apply during business hours and are approved, documents can be signed the same day and funds deposited within 24 hours. You do not need to prove the nature of the emergency. Applications made on weekends or holidays are reviewed the next business day. The fastest products are term loans, lines of credit, bridge capital, working capital and revenue-based financing; SBA financing is not a fast option.

## Turned down by a bank?
Source: https://sbgfunding.com/business-financing/fast-business-loans/

Most businesses SBG funds have been declined by at least one bank. Bank declines usually come from rigid credit thresholds, documentation requirements or slow timelines rather than the health of the business. If the business has $150,000+ in annual revenue and at least 6 months of operating history, SBG can likely help regardless of the bank's answer.
